Basic Principles of Organic Chemistry

Basic Principles of Organic Chemistry

Key concepts

  • Carbon forms four bonds in many organic compounds. Common bonding descriptions include sp³, sp² and sp hybridisation.
  • A nucleophile donates an electron pair; an electrophile accepts an electron pair. Bond cleavage can be homolytic or heterolytic.
  • Inductive effects act through sigma bonds; resonance describes delocalisation represented by contributing structures.

Worked connection

In methane, carbon is described as sp³ with tetrahedral geometry; in ethene each carbon is sp²; in ethyne each is sp.

Watch out

Resonance contributors are not separate molecules rapidly interconverting. The actual structure is delocalised.

Concept walkthrough and worked checkpoints

Use these seven checkpoints to connect the core ideas, practise applications and identify assumptions. Try each before opening its solution.

1. An electrophile is an electron-pair:

Acceptor

Electrophiles accept an electron pair during bond formation; they may be charged or neutral.

2. Carbon hybridisation in ethene is commonly described as:

sp²

Each carbon has three sigma-bond directions and a remaining p orbital for the pi bond.

3. Carbon hybridisation in ethyne is commonly described as:

sp

Two sigma directions give linear sp geometry; two remaining p orbitals support the two pi bonds.

4. Heterolytic bond cleavage assigns both bonding electrons to:

One fragment

One fragment gains the pair, commonly creating an anion and a cation.

5. The inductive effect is transmitted mainly through:

Sigma bonds

Bond polarisation is transmitted through the sigma framework and typically weakens with distance.

6. Resonance contributors describe:

One delocalised structure

Contributors are representations; the actual electronic structure is the resonance hybrid.

7. Structural isomers have the same molecular formula but different:

Connectivity

Changing atom connectivity creates constitutional or structural isomers.

Practice questions and explanations

Try each question before opening its answer.

1. A nucleophile is an electron-pair:

  1. Acceptor
  2. Donor
  3. Neutron source
  4. Proton only
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Donor

Nucleophiles donate electron pairs to an electrophilic centre.

2. Carbon hybridisation in methane is:

  1. sp
  2. sp²
  3. sp³
  4. dsp²
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. sp³

Methane has four sigma bonds in tetrahedral geometry.

3. Homolytic cleavage of a covalent bond produces:

  1. Two radicals
  2. Only one cation
  3. Only one anion
  4. Only neutrons
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Two radicals

Each fragment takes one electron from the bond.

4. An electrophile is an electron-pair:

  1. Acceptor
  2. Donor
  3. Only a neutron
  4. Only an uncharged radical
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Acceptor

Electrophiles accept an electron pair during bond formation; they may be charged or neutral.

5. Carbon hybridisation in ethene is commonly described as:

  1. sp²
  2. sp³
  3. sp
  4. d²sp³
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. sp²

Each carbon has three sigma-bond directions and a remaining p orbital for the pi bond.

6. Carbon hybridisation in ethyne is commonly described as:

  1. sp
  2. sp²
  3. sp³
  4. dsp²
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. sp

Two sigma directions give linear sp geometry; two remaining p orbitals support the two pi bonds.

7. Heterolytic bond cleavage assigns both bonding electrons to:

  1. One fragment
  2. Both fragments equally
  3. Neither fragment
  4. Only a neutron
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. One fragment

One fragment gains the pair, commonly creating an anion and a cation.

8. The inductive effect is transmitted mainly through:

  1. Sigma bonds
  2. Only free neutrons
  3. Only hydrogen gas
  4. Only isolated photons
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Sigma bonds

Bond polarisation is transmitted through the sigma framework and typically weakens with distance.

9. Resonance contributors describe:

  1. One delocalised structure
  2. Separate molecules in rapid equilibrium
  3. Different elements
  4. Only geometric conformers
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. One delocalised structure

Contributors are representations; the actual electronic structure is the resonance hybrid.

10. Structural isomers have the same molecular formula but different:

  1. Connectivity
  2. Atomic numbers of each element
  3. Total element composition
  4. Total electron count in every neutral formula necessarily
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Connectivity

Changing atom connectivity creates constitutional or structural isomers.

11. In homolytic bond cleavage, the bonding electron pair is:

  1. Given entirely to one fragment
  2. Destroyed
  3. Converted to protons
  4. Split equally between fragments
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Split equally between fragments

Each fragment receives one electron, commonly forming radicals.

12. In heterolytic bond cleavage, both bonding electrons go to:

  1. The nucleus of each fragment
  2. An unrelated neutron
  3. One fragment
  4. Neither fragment
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. One fragment

Unequal electron allocation commonly produces a cation and an anion.

13. A carbon atom with a positive charge and an incomplete octet is commonly a:

  1. Carbon isotope only
  2. Carbocation
  3. Carbanion
  4. Neutral noble gas
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Carbocation

A carbocation is an electron-deficient positively charged carbon centre.

14. A negatively charged carbon species is a:

  1. Carbanion
  2. Carbocation
  3. Neutron
  4. Metal cation
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Carbanion

A carbanion carries an extra electron pair or negative charge on carbon.

15. An electrophile commonly seeks:

  1. A neutron pair
  2. Only an existing positive charge
  3. An empty nucleus
  4. An electron pair
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. An electron pair

Electron-deficient electrophiles accept electron density from nucleophiles.

16. A nucleophile commonly donates:

  1. A neutron
  2. Its entire nucleus
  3. An electron pair
  4. A proton in every case
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. An electron pair

Nucleophiles are electron-pair donors in bond-forming reactions.

17. Which is a common nucleophile?

  1. AlCl₃
  2. OH⁻
  3. BF₃
  4. H⁺
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. OH⁻

Hydroxide has lone-pair electron density available for donation.

18. Which is a common Lewis-acid electrophile?

  1. BF₃
  2. OH⁻
  3. CN⁻
  4. NH₃
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. BF₃

Boron trifluoride has an electron-deficient boron centre able to accept a pair.

19. The inductive effect is transmitted primarily through:

  1. Only pi-light emission
  2. Only nuclear reactions
  3. Only hydrogen isotopes
  4. Sigma bonds
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Sigma bonds

Polarisation propagates through sigma bonds and decreases with distance.

20. Resonance requires contributors with the same:

  1. Total neutron count only
  2. Different numbers of atoms
  3. Atomic connectivity and nuclear framework
  4. Electron positions always
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Atomic connectivity and nuclear framework

Resonance changes electron placement while keeping the atoms fixed.

21. Hyperconjugation involves interaction of a sigma bond with:

  1. No electron system
  2. An adjacent suitable orbital
  3. A neutron shell
  4. Only an isolated distant nucleus
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. An adjacent suitable orbital

Sigma electrons can delocalise into a neighbouring p or pi system.

22. For simple alkyl carbocations, the usual stability order is:

  1. Tertiary > secondary > primary
  2. Primary > secondary > tertiary
  3. All exactly equal
  4. Methyl > tertiary always
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Tertiary > secondary > primary

Alkyl electron donation and hyperconjugation commonly stabilise more substituted carbocations.

23. An allylic carbocation is stabilised mainly by:

  1. Loss of all electrons
  2. Only atomic mass
  3. A filled neutron shell
  4. Resonance delocalisation
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Resonance delocalisation

The positive charge can be distributed across the adjacent pi system.

24. An sp-hybridised carbon holds its bonding electrons relatively tightly because it has:

  1. Only d character
  2. No valence orbitals
  3. Greater s character
  4. No s character
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Greater s character

An sp orbital has 50% s character, increasing electron density close to the nucleus.

25. Geometrical isomerism around an alkene requires suitable substituents and restricted:

  1. Rotation about every single bond
  2. Rotation about the double bond
  3. Electron count in the nucleus
  4. Solubility of all reactants
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Rotation about the double bond

Restricted rotation allows distinct substituent arrangements when each double-bond carbon has two different groups.

26. Chain isomerism concerns a change in:

  1. Carbon skeleton
  2. Only neutron number
  3. Only temperature
  4. Only spatial rotation of the same bond
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Carbon skeleton

Different branching patterns can give different carbon frameworks.

27. Position isomerism concerns a different position of a:

  1. Nucleus inside an atom
  2. Solvent container
  3. Neutron in a nucleus
  4. Functional group or multiple bond
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Functional group or multiple bond

The functional feature occupies a different location on the same basic skeleton.

28. Alcohols and ethers with the same molecular formula can illustrate:

  1. Radioactive isomerism
  2. Identical functional groups
  3. Functional-group isomerism
  4. Only conformational change
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Functional-group isomerism

Alcohol and ether connect oxygen differently and constitute different functional classes.

29. An elimination reaction typically forms a multiple bond by:

  1. Removing the whole carbon skeleton
  2. Removing groups from neighbouring atoms
  3. Adding only neutrons
  4. Breaking no chemical bonds
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Removing groups from neighbouring atoms

Loss of suitable groups from adjacent atoms increases bond multiplicity.

30. A substitution reaction replaces:

  1. One atom or group with another
  2. Every nucleus with a neutron
  3. Only a pi bond with light
  4. The entire molecule with vacuum
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. One atom or group with another

The defining change exchanges a substituent while retaining the underlying framework.
