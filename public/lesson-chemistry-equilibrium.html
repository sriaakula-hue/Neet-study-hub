Equilibrium

Acids, bases and pH

In the Brønsted–Lowry description, an acid donates a proton and a base accepts a proton.

pH ≈ −log₁₀[H⁺]

This concentration expression approximates pH for dilute aqueous solutions; the rigorous definition uses hydrogen-ion activity.

Worked example

If [H⁺] is 10⁻³ mol/L in a dilute solution, pH is approximately 3.

Temperature matters

In water at 25 °C, neutral pH is approximately 7 and pH + pOH is approximately 14. Neutral pH changes with temperature.

Dynamic equilibrium

At equilibrium, forward and reverse reaction rates are equal, while concentrations remain constant rather than necessarily equal. A catalyst accelerates both directions without changing the equilibrium constant. Temperature can change the equilibrium constant.

Concept walkthrough and worked checkpoints

Use these seven checkpoints to connect the core ideas, practise applications and identify assumptions. Try each before opening its solution.

1. At chemical equilibrium, forward and reverse reaction rates are:

Equal

Dynamic equilibrium continues in both directions at equal rates, keeping macroscopic concentrations constant.

2. A catalyst changes an equilibrium constant at fixed temperature by:

Not changing it

A catalyst changes the path and speeds both directions but does not change thermodynamic equilibrium.

3. For an exothermic forward reaction, raising temperature generally favours:

The reverse direction

Adding heat favours the heat-consuming direction; the equilibrium constant for the exothermic forward reaction generally decreases.

4. For N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, increasing pressure by reducing volume at fixed T favours:

NH₃ formation

The product side has fewer gaseous moles: two versus four.

5. A dilute aqueous solution has [H⁺] = 10⁻⁵ mol/L. Approximate pH is:

5

Using the concentration approximation pH ≈ −log₁₀[H⁺], the result is 5.

6. A buffer resists moderate pH changes because it commonly contains:

A weak acid and its conjugate base

The acid/base pair can consume modest added base or acid, within the buffer capacity.

7. Adding a common ion to a sparingly soluble salt generally:

Reduces its solubility

Under suitable ideal conditions, the common-ion shift suppresses dissolution; other reactions or activity effects can complicate real cases.

Practice questions and explanations

Try each question before opening its answer.

1. For a dilute solution with [H⁺] = 10⁻³ mol/L, the approximate pH is:

  1. 11
  2. 7
  3. −3
  4. 3
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. 3

pH ≈ −log₁₀(10⁻³) = 3.

2. A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a:

  1. Proton acceptor
  2. Proton donor
  3. Neutron donor
  4. Salt only
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Proton donor

Acids donate protons; bases accept them.

3. Neutral water at 25 °C has pH approximately:

  1. 0
  2. 3
  3. 7
  4. 14
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. 7

At 25 °C, equal hydrogen- and hydroxide-ion concentrations give neutral pH near 7.

4. At chemical equilibrium, forward and reverse reaction rates are:

  1. Equal
  2. Both necessarily zero
  3. Always different
  4. Equal to concentrations
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Equal

Dynamic equilibrium continues in both directions at equal rates, keeping macroscopic concentrations constant.

5. A catalyst changes an equilibrium constant at fixed temperature by:

  1. Not changing it
  2. Always increasing it
  3. Always decreasing it
  4. Making it zero
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Not changing it

A catalyst changes the path and speeds both directions but does not change thermodynamic equilibrium.

6. For an exothermic forward reaction, raising temperature generally favours:

  1. The reverse direction
  2. The forward direction always
  3. No shift ever
  4. Complete removal of reactants
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. The reverse direction

Adding heat favours the heat-consuming direction; the equilibrium constant for the exothermic forward reaction generally decreases.

7. For N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, increasing pressure by reducing volume at fixed T favours:

  1. NH₃ formation
  2. The side with four gas moles
  3. Neither side under ideal assumptions
  4. Only nitrogen formation
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. NH₃ formation

The product side has fewer gaseous moles: two versus four.

8. A dilute aqueous solution has [H⁺] = 10⁻⁵ mol/L. Approximate pH is:

  2. 14
  3. 5

9. A buffer resists moderate pH changes because it commonly contains:

  1. A weak acid and its conjugate base
  2. Only pure water
  3. Only a noble gas
  4. An insoluble metal alone
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. A weak acid and its conjugate base

The acid/base pair can consume modest added base or acid, within the buffer capacity.

10. Adding a common ion to a sparingly soluble salt generally:

  1. Reduces its solubility
  2. Always increases solubility
  3. Eliminates equilibrium
  4. Changes every ion into atoms
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Reduces its solubility

Under suitable ideal conditions, the common-ion shift suppresses dissolution; other reactions or activity effects can complicate real cases.

  2. 6

Show answer and explanation

At 25°C, pH+pOH≈14 under the dilute aqueous approximation. Answer: 12.

13. A weak acid has K_a=10⁻⁵ and concentration 0.04 mol/L. Assuming small dissociation and negligible water ionisation, [H⁺] is approximately:

  1. 0.000316228 mol/L
  2. 0.000948683 mol/L
  3. 0.00126491 mol/L
  4. 0.000632456 mol/L
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. 0.000632456 mol/L

For a monoprotic weak acid with small dissociation, [H⁺]≈√(K_aC). Answer: 0.000632456 mol/L.

14. For an ideal acid buffer, pK_a=4 and [A⁻]/[HA]=10^2. Approximate pH is:

  1. 9
  2. 12
  3. 6
  4. 3
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. 6

Henderson–Hasselbalch gives pH=pK_a+log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA]) under buffer assumptions. Answer: 6.
