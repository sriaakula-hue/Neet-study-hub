The Living World

The Living World

EXPANDED REVISION · LEARNING OBJECTIVES

Distinguish identification, naming and classification; interpret a scientific name; and move confidently through taxonomic ranks.

1. A name is not a classification

Think of a library: recognising a particular book, giving it a catalogue label and placing it on a shelf are separate tasks. Similarly, identification recognises an organism, nomenclature supplies its accepted scientific name, and classification places it in related groups. Taxonomy brings these tasks together. Systematics additionally studies relationships, including evolutionary history.

2. Read the two-word name

In Homo sapiens, Homo is the genus and sapiens is the specific epithet. The epithet alone does not identify the complete species. Print both words in italics, capitalise only the genus, and underline the words separately when handwriting them. Scientific naming reduces confusion caused by multiple local names.

3. Rank versus group

A category is a rank, such as family. A taxon is an actual group at that rank, such as Hominidae. The usual sequence from narrow to broad is species → genus → family → order → class → phylum or division → kingdom. Broader categories include more groups; narrower categories usually unite organisms with more closely shared traits.

4. Worked comparison

Two organisms in the same genus also share its family and higher ranks. Two organisms in the same family need not be in the same genus. Treat the hierarchy as nested boxes: sharing a small box means sharing its outer boxes, but sharing an outer box does not require sharing every inner box.

5. Avoid the trap

A dominant-looking trait or a familiar local name is insufficient for reliable identification. Classification draws on multiple characters. Reproduction is also not required of every individual for that individual to be living: sterile organisms are still alive.

Self-check

Explain why “same family” does not imply “same species”. Then write a correctly formatted example of a species name and identify each component.

Practice questions and explanations

Try each question before opening its answer.

1. Which taxonomic category is the most specific?

  1. Kingdom
  2. Family
  3. Genus
  4. Species
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Species

Species is the lowest, most specific category in the usual hierarchy.

2. In Homo sapiens, Homo names the:

  1. Family
  2. Genus
  3. Order
  4. Kingdom
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Genus

The first word is the genus.

3. Which category lies immediately above genus?

  1. Species
  2. Family
  3. Class
  4. Kingdom
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Family

The sequence is species → genus → family.

4. Two organisms belong to the same genus. They must also share the same:

  1. Species
  2. Family
  3. Population
  4. Individual age
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Family

A genus is nested within a family, but can include multiple species.

5. Two organisms share a family. Which is necessarily true?

  1. They share a genus
  2. They share higher taxonomic ranks
  3. They are identical
  4. They can always interbreed
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. They share higher taxonomic ranks

They share the enclosing higher categories, but may belong to different genera.

6. Which correctly describes a specific epithet?

  1. The full two-word species name
  2. The second component of a binomial
  3. Always the family
  4. Always capitalised
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. The second component of a binomial

A complete binomial includes the genus and the specific epithet.

7. Recognising which known organism a specimen represents is:

  1. Identification
  2. Fermentation
  3. Respiration
  4. Only reproduction
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Identification

Identification assigns the specimen to a recognised identity.

8. Which sequence goes from narrow to broad?

  1. Family → genus → species
  2. Species → genus → family
  3. Kingdom → species → genus
  4. Order → species → family
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Species → genus → family

Species is nested in genus, and genus in family.

9. In printed scientific species names, which is the usual format?

  1. Both words capitalised
  2. Genus capitalised, epithet lowercase, both italicised
  3. Both uppercase and never italicised
  4. Epithet first
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Genus capitalised, epithet lowercase, both italicised

This is the usual binomial formatting convention.

10. Why do scientific names help communication?

  1. They replace all biological observations
  2. They reduce ambiguity across local languages
  3. They show every ecological trait
  4. They identify age
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. They reduce ambiguity across local languages

A standardised name helps people refer to the same organism consistently.
