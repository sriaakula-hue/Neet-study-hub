The Living World

The Living World

EXPANDED REVISION · LEARNING OBJECTIVES

Distinguish identification, naming and classification; interpret a scientific name; and move confidently through taxonomic ranks.

1. A name is not a classification

Think of a library: recognising a particular book, giving it a catalogue label and placing it on a shelf are separate tasks. Similarly, identification recognises an organism, nomenclature supplies its accepted scientific name, and classification places it in related groups. Taxonomy brings these tasks together. Systematics additionally studies relationships, including evolutionary history.

2. Read the two-word name

In Homo sapiens, Homo is the genus and sapiens is the specific epithet. The epithet alone does not identify the complete species. Print both words in italics, capitalise only the genus, and underline the words separately when handwriting them. Scientific naming reduces confusion caused by multiple local names.

3. Rank versus group

A category is a rank, such as family. A taxon is an actual group at that rank, such as Hominidae. The usual sequence from narrow to broad is species → genus → family → order → class → phylum or division → kingdom. Broader categories include more groups; narrower categories usually unite organisms with more closely shared traits.

4. Worked comparison

Two organisms in the same genus also share its family and higher ranks. Two organisms in the same family need not be in the same genus. Treat the hierarchy as nested boxes: sharing a small box means sharing its outer boxes, but sharing an outer box does not require sharing every inner box.

5. Avoid the trap

A dominant-looking trait or a familiar local name is insufficient for reliable identification. Classification draws on multiple characters. Reproduction is also not required of every individual for that individual to be living: sterile organisms are still alive.

Self-check

Explain why “same family” does not imply “same species”. Then write a correctly formatted example of a species name and identify each component.

Practice questions and explanations

Try each question before opening its answer.

1. Which taxonomic category is the most specific?

  1. Kingdom
  2. Family
  3. Genus
  4. Species
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Species

Species is the lowest, most specific category in the usual hierarchy.

2. In Homo sapiens, Homo names the:

  1. Family
  2. Genus
  3. Order
  4. Kingdom
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Genus

The first word is the genus.

3. Which category lies immediately above genus?

  1. Species
  2. Family
  3. Class
  4. Kingdom
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Family

The sequence is species → genus → family.

4. Two organisms belong to the same genus. They must also share the same:

  1. Species
  2. Family
  3. Population
  4. Individual age
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Family

A genus is nested within a family, but can include multiple species.

5. Two organisms share a family. Which is necessarily true?

  1. They share a genus
  2. They share higher taxonomic ranks
  3. They are identical
  4. They can always interbreed
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. They share higher taxonomic ranks

They share the enclosing higher categories, but may belong to different genera.

6. Which correctly describes a specific epithet?

  1. The full two-word species name
  2. The second component of a binomial
  3. Always the family
  4. Always capitalised
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. The second component of a binomial

A complete binomial includes the genus and the specific epithet.

7. Recognising which known organism a specimen represents is:

  1. Identification
  2. Fermentation
  3. Respiration
  4. Only reproduction
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Identification

Identification assigns the specimen to a recognised identity.

8. Which sequence goes from narrow to broad?

  1. Family → genus → species
  2. Species → genus → family
  3. Kingdom → species → genus
  4. Order → species → family
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Species → genus → family

Species is nested in genus, and genus in family.

9. In printed scientific species names, which is the usual format?

  1. Both words capitalised
  2. Genus capitalised, epithet lowercase, both italicised
  3. Both uppercase and never italicised
  4. Epithet first
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Genus capitalised, epithet lowercase, both italicised

This is the usual binomial formatting convention.

10. Why do scientific names help communication?

  1. They replace all biological observations
  2. They reduce ambiguity across local languages
  3. They show every ecological trait
  4. They identify age
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. They reduce ambiguity across local languages

A standardised name helps people refer to the same organism consistently.

11. Binomial nomenclature assigns an organism a:

  1. Genus name and specific epithet
  2. Kingdom name only
  3. Family name and isotope number
  4. Three unrelated numbers
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Genus name and specific epithet

The two-part name combines genus with the species epithet.

12. In a written scientific name, the genus begins with a:

  1. Lowercase letter always
  2. Numeral
  3. Chemical symbol
  4. Capital letter
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Capital letter

The genus is capitalised while the specific epithet begins lowercase.

13. A scientific name is conventionally printed in:

  1. Quotation marks only
  2. Superscript only
  3. Italics
  4. All capitals
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Italics

Italics distinguish the formal name; handwriting convention underlines its words separately.

14. The smallest standard taxonomic category among these is:

  1. Order
  2. Species
  3. Genus
  4. Family
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Species

Species is the basic unit in the conventional hierarchy.

15. A genus contains one or more related:

  1. Species
  2. Orders
  3. Classes
  4. Kingdoms
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Species

Related species are grouped within a genus.

16. A family commonly groups related:

  1. Kingdoms
  2. Phyla
  3. Domains only
  4. Genera
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Genera

The family rank lies above genus and below order.

17. An order commonly groups related:

  1. Kingdoms
  2. Domains
  3. Families
  4. Species only without families
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Families

Order is above family in the standard hierarchy.

18. The category immediately above order in the usual hierarchy is:

  1. Family
  2. Class
  3. Genus
  4. Species
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Class

Class contains related orders.

19. The category immediately above class in the usual hierarchy is:

  1. Phylum or division
  2. Species
  3. Genus
  4. Family
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Phylum or division

Phylum is commonly used for animals and division traditionally for plants.

20. As one moves towards broader taxonomic categories, shared characteristics generally:

  1. Become more detailed without exception
  2. Stay identical for all organisms
  3. Disappear at every rank
  4. Become fewer
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Become fewer

Broader groups contain more diverse organisms and fewer narrowly shared traits.

21. Taxonomy includes identification, nomenclature and:

  1. Only digestion
  2. Only mutation
  3. Classification
  4. Only respiration
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Classification

These activities organise and name biological diversity.

22. Systematics additionally emphasises:

  1. Only habitat temperature
  2. Evolutionary relationships
  3. Only body mass
  4. Only commercial use
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Evolutionary relationships

Systematics studies diversity in relation to biological history and relationships.

23. A taxon is a:

  1. Biological group at a recognised rank
  2. Single chemical bond
  3. Fixed laboratory instrument
  4. Type of ATP
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Biological group at a recognised rank

Species, genus and other ranked groups are examples of taxa.

24. A herbarium stores mainly:

  1. Only living fish
  2. Only frozen enzymes
  3. Only rocks
  4. Preserved pressed plant specimens
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Preserved pressed plant specimens

Dried mounted specimens support plant identification and documentation.

25. A botanical garden mainly maintains:

  1. Only bacteria in every bed
  2. Only textbooks
  3. Living plant collections
  4. Only dead animal skins
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Living plant collections

Labelled living plants aid study and conservation.

26. A museum collection may preserve:

  1. Only plasmids
  2. Animal and plant specimens
  3. Only live elephants
  4. Only climate measurements
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Animal and plant specimens

Specimens and associated records support biological reference work.

27. A taxonomic key commonly works through contrasting:

  1. Character choices in paired leads
  2. Only molecular masses
  3. Only colours without context
  4. Only dates
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Character choices in paired leads

Dichotomous alternatives guide identification step by step.

28. A monograph provides a detailed account of a:

  1. Single universal temperature
  2. Single atom
  3. Single currency
  4. Particular taxonomic group
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Particular taxonomic group

It gathers extensive information about a selected group.

29. Biological growth by itself is not a unique sign of life because:

  1. Growth always requires photosynthesis
  2. Every crystal reproduces genetically
  3. Nonliving objects can enlarge by accumulation
  4. Living things never grow
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Nonliving objects can enlarge by accumulation

Crystals can increase size externally, unlike regulated biological growth.

30. Metabolism comprises the organism's:

  1. Only external shape
  2. Chemical transformations
  3. Only movement across land
  4. Only reproduction
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Chemical transformations

Anabolism and catabolism together constitute metabolic activity.
